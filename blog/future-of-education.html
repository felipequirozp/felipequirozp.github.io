The future of education isn’t built with technology alone.

A reflection from País Digital Summit on AI, partnerships and why transforming education depends as much on people, institutions and the ability to execute together as it does on technology.

País Digital Summit — Future of Education · Santiago, Chile
One idea I still come back to. Technology, institutions and collaboration have to move together if we want innovation to actually reach people.

When we talk about the future of education, it is easy to move the conversation straight to technology: artificial intelligence, new platforms, automation or more personalized learning experiences. All of that matters. But none of it transforms a system on its own.

That was one of the ideas that stayed with me after attending País Digital Summit: Future of Education. The event brought together people from government, academia and international organizations to discuss the challenges and opportunities of digital transformation in education.

At the time, I was leading strategic partnerships at TetherEd, so I looked at these conversations through a very concrete question: how do we make sure a good idea, a technology or a policy does not stay in a presentation, but actually reaches students, families and education communities?

Technology does not transform a system on its own.

A tool can be extraordinary and still have little impact if it cannot fit into the context where it needs to work. In education, that means understanding institutions, processes, incentives, capabilities, regulation, adoption and, above all, the people who have to use it.

During the summit, experts such as Wayne Holmes from University College London shared perspectives on AI, learning and how these technologies are beginning to shape the future of education. The AI conversation was relevant, but it also reinforced something broader for me: the challenge is not only to build better tools, but to create the conditions for those tools to generate real value.

Educational innovation does not happen when a new technology appears. It happens when that technology becomes a real capability of the system.

Partnerships are infrastructure too.

We tend to think of infrastructure as platforms, data or connectivity. But in complex ecosystems there is another kind of infrastructure that matters just as much: the relationships between the people and organizations that have to make things happen.

Governments, schools, universities, startups, foundations, companies and international organizations can look at the same problem from very different angles. When those actors work in isolation, even strong initiatives can end up duplicated, moving slowly or failing to achieve adoption.

That is why partnership work has always interested me so much. It is not only about “closing partnerships.” It is about connecting different capabilities around a shared problem and finding a concrete way to turn that coordination into execution.

  • A startup can bring speed, product thinking and the ability to experiment.
  • An education institution understands the real context where the solution has to work.
  • The public sector can provide scale, legitimacy and continuity.
  • Academia and specialized organizations can contribute evidence, research and perspective.

The value appears when those pieces stop operating as separate initiatives and start behaving like a system.

AI makes this conversation even more important.

The speed at which artificial intelligence is advancing makes it tempting to look at education through a purely technological lens. But the more powerful the technology becomes, the more important it becomes to decide how, where and why to use it.

In education, the interesting question is not only what an AI model can do. It is also who will have access, how the work of teachers and teams changes, which processes can improve and what conditions are needed for technology to expand opportunity instead of widening existing gaps.

Scale does not start with more users.

One of the things I learned working in EdTech is that scale is not simply about reaching more people. First, product, adoption, partnerships and operations need to keep working as the context changes.

The same applies to education transformation. To move from one strong isolated experience to broader change, you need repeatable processes, aligned stakeholders and a way to learn quickly from what happens on the ground.

That is why spaces like this matter. Not because a conference will solve the challenges in education, but because they create connections between people who are usually working on different parts of the same problem.

What I took away.

After that summit, one idea became much clearer to me: the future of education will probably involve much more technology, but it will not be built by technologists alone.

It will require product, public policy, education, research, operations, partnerships and people who can translate between those worlds.

The opportunity is to connect those pieces better. Because digital transformation stops being a promise when it becomes a better experience for the people on the other side of the system.
