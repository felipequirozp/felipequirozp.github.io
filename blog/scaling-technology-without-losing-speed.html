Scaling technology without losing speed.

Reflections from Tech Founders AMA on scalability, technical debt, engineering leadership and how AI is becoming part of the development workflow.

Tech Founders AMA · Buk · Santiago, Chile · September 2026

One idea I kept thinking about. Scaling is not just a technical challenge. Every architecture decision eventually becomes a product, team and business decision too.

At Tech Founders AMA, I had the chance to join a conversation about how technology companies scale their architecture, teams and products without losing speed or focus.

Sebastián Hevia Ovalle from AgendaPro, Lukas Zorich from Fintoc and Felipe Sateler from Buk shared practical experiences around scalability, technical debt, engineering leadership and the integration of AI into development workflows. Rodrigo Oyarzún, Head of Product at Buk Finanzas, moderated the session.

What made the conversation especially interesting was that the topics were not treated as isolated engineering problems. They were discussed as decisions that shape how a company builds, prioritizes and operates as it grows.

Scale changes what a “good decision” looks like.

A decision that is perfectly reasonable at an early stage can become a constraint later. The opposite is also true: over-engineering too early can slow a team down before there is enough evidence that the complexity is necessary.

That tension showed up repeatedly during the conversation. Scaling is not about eliminating trade-offs. It is about getting better at understanding which trade-offs are worth making at each stage of the company.

The challenge is not to avoid technical debt at all costs. It is to know which debt is helping you move faster and which debt is starting to slow the company down.

Technical debt is also a prioritization problem.

Technical debt is often discussed as if it were only an engineering concern. But deciding when to take it on, when to pay it down and how much of it the organization can absorb is ultimately a prioritization decision.

Product pressure, customer needs, team capacity and architecture all compete for the same limited attention. That makes the conversation about technical debt much closer to product strategy than it may seem from the outside.

What stood out

The most useful discussions were the ones that connected technical choices with speed, product quality and the ability of teams to keep executing as the organization grows.

Engineering leadership becomes a systems problem.

As teams grow, maintaining a high bar is no longer just about hiring strong engineers. It also depends on how decisions are made, how context travels across teams and how much autonomy people can have without creating unnecessary fragmentation.

Culture, tooling and operating principles start to matter as much as individual technical decisions. The system around the team becomes part of the product development infrastructure.

AI is moving into the workflow, not just the demo.

Another recurring topic was the integration of AI into development. The interesting part was not the novelty of using AI tools, but how they are becoming embedded in everyday engineering workflows.

That shift changes the question from “should teams use AI?” to “where does AI actually improve the workflow, and how do we integrate it without lowering the quality bar?”

The same principle applies here as with architecture and technical debt: the value comes from making the tool part of a better system, not from using it simply because it is available.

Product and technology get closer as companies scale.

My main takeaway from the event was that product and engineering decisions become increasingly intertwined as a company grows.

Architecture affects delivery speed. Technical debt affects roadmap flexibility. Team structure affects product quality. AI tools affect how work gets done. None of those decisions live entirely inside one function.

Hearing these experiences first-hand was a good reminder that scaling well is less about finding a perfect architecture or process and more about building the judgment to evolve both as the company changes.
