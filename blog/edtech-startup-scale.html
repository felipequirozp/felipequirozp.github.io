What building an edtech startup taught me about scale.

Scaling is not just growth. It is making product, adoption, partnerships and operations keep working as the context changes.

TetherEd · Education & technology
One idea that stayed with me. Growth becomes scale when the system stops depending on specific people.

Working at TetherEd changed how I think about growth quite a bit. From the outside, scaling a startup can look mostly like an acquisition problem. From the inside, you quickly realize it is a systems problem.

The challenge TetherEd was trying to solve.

TetherEd developed user-centered guidance products for choice and assignment environments, including school choice, college admissions and other high-impact matching markets.

The idea was to make public systems that are often difficult to understand and navigate much simpler: helping families make better-informed decisions while giving institutions the measurement infrastructure needed to understand what was actually working at scale.

That vision was closely connected to the background of Christopher Neilson, Founder & CEO of TetherEd, Professor of Economics and Global Affairs at Yale University, and also the founder of ConsiliumBots.

The bet behind TetherEd was that a small, specialized product team, working closely with researchers and public policy leaders, could build better interfaces for systems that had historically been designed around institutional logic rather than the user experience.

The timing was interesting too. Centralized choice and assignment systems were becoming increasingly common, but the experience of the people navigating those processes had not always evolved at the same pace. That created a clear opportunity to apply product thinking to complex public infrastructure.

When you work with schools, institutions and public organizations, different realities show up fast: their own internal processes, teams with different levels of digital adoption, calendars, administrative constraints and priorities that change from one territory to another.

That means adding more users or institutions is only part of the problem. The real question is whether what you built keeps working when the context changes.

Scaling is not just growth. It is making something keep working when the context changes.

Scaling is not distribution.

You can bring in more institutions, open new markets and increase usage without having built a truly scalable operation.

Early on, many things work because knowledge lives implicitly inside the team. One person knows how to explain the product, someone else remembers what each institution needs and another person resolves every exception manually.

The problem appears when volume increases. What depended on specific people starts becoming a bottleneck.

Scale requires product, communication and processes to absorb part of that complexity.

Adoption is part of the product.

One of the clearest lessons was understanding that adoption does not come after the product. It is part of the product.

If an institution needs too much support to adopt a tool, there is probably still friction the system has not absorbed.

Sometimes that friction lives in the interface. Other times it lives in implementation, approvals, missing information or the way the product interacts with processes that existed long before it arrived.

One idea that stayed with me

When adoption depends too heavily on specific people, there may be growth, but there is not yet real scale.

Partnerships can be infrastructure too.

Working in education also changed the way I thought about partnerships. I stopped seeing them as a purely commercial function.

In complex markets, a strong partnership can provide distribution, legitimacy, local context, access to communities or operating capacity that a startup could not build alone at the same speed.

That changes the question from “how many partnerships do we have?” to “what can we do now that we could not do before?”.

A partnership matters because of the capability it enables afterwards, not only because an agreement gets signed.

Operations make scale real.

Follow-up, training, coordination, support and hundreds of small decisions end up determining whether a strategy actually works in practice.

That operational layer is often less visible than product or growth, but it is where many good ideas either hold together or break.

It is also one of the reasons I started thinking about product in a broader way. A feature can be well designed and still fail if the system around it does not allow people to adopt it.

Product, growth, partnerships and operations live together.

Over time, it became harder for me to separate those four functions.

Product defines what capability exists. Growth helps it reach more people. Partnerships can unlock context, distribution or trust. Operations makes sure the whole thing keeps working once volume, exceptions and new scenarios start appearing.

In practice, all four live inside the same system.

And maybe that is the most interesting part of scale: not optimizing one function, but helping the whole system learn to work better as it grows.
